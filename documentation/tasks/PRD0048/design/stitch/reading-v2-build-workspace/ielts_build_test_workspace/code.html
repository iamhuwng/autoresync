The concept of a pen that carries its own ink supply within it was a revolutionary idea in the 19th century. Early attempts often resulted in leaks, clogs, and inconsistent ink flow. It wasn't until the development of the ballpoint pen that a truly reliable and portable writing instrument became widely available.

The core mechanism of a ballpoint pen involves a tiny sphere, usually made of brass, steel, or tungsten carbide, housed in a socket at the tip of the pen. As the pen moves across paper, the ball rotates, picking up ink from the reservoir and depositing it onto the writing surface. This seemingly simple mechanism required precise engineering to perfect.

László Bíró, a Hungarian newspaper editor, is widely credited with inventing the modern ballpoint pen. Frustrated by the smudging of fountain pen ink and the time it took to dry, Bíró noticed that the ink used in newspaper printing dried quickly and left a smudge-free mark. He enlisted the help of his brother György, a chemist, to develop a viscous ink suitable for a pen.
