The History of the Bicycle

The bicycle is a remarkable piece of engineering that has undergone numerous transformations since its inception. While the exact origins are debated, the earliest verifiable ancestor was the Draisienne, invented by Karl Drais in 1817. This "running machine" lacked pedals and was propelled by the rider pushing their feet against the ground. It featured a rudimentary form of [21], allowing the front wheel to be pivoted for direction.

Significant progress occurred in the 1860s with the introduction of the velocipede, also known as the "boneshaker." This model attached pedals directly to the front wheel, a major innovation, though its stiff wooden wheels and iron frame made for a jarring ride. To increase speed, inventors began enlarging the front wheel, leading to the iconic Penny Farthing. However, these machines were inherently dangerous due to their high center of gravity.

The true revolution in bicycle design arrived in the 1880s with the "safety bicycle." John Kemp Starley's Rover, introduced in 1885, established the modern template: two wheels of equal size, a diamond-shaped frame, and a [22] to transmit power from the pedals to the rear wheel. This design vastly improved stability and efficiency, opening cycling to a much wider demographic.
