The Evolution of City Planning

Urban planning is a technical and political process concerned with the development and design of land use and the built environment. It encompasses the physical layout of human settlements, ranging from tiny villages to sprawling megacities. The primary goal is to ensure the orderly development of settlements and satellite communities, addressing aspects such as transportation, communications, and distribution networks.

Figure 1: Grid patterns in early 20th-century expansion.

In the late 19th century, the Garden City movement, initiated by Sir Ebenezer Howard, sought to combine the best features of town and country living. This approach advocated for self-contained communities surrounded by greenbelts, containing proportionate areas of residences, industry, and agriculture. It was a direct response to the overcrowding and pollution characteristic of industrial cities at the time.

Modernist planning, which gained prominence in the mid-20th century, favored strict zoning and the separation of land uses. Planners like Le Corbusier envisioned 'Radiant Cities' characterized by high-density skyscrapers set within park-like environments, connected by expansive highways. However, this model later faced criticism for destroying traditional street life and fostering automobile dependency.

Today, contemporary urban planning often emphasizes sustainable development, mixed-use zoning, and transit-oriented design. The focus has shifted towards creating walkable neighborhoods, reducing carbon footprints, and ensuring equitable access to urban amenities, reflecting a growing awareness of environmental and social issues.
