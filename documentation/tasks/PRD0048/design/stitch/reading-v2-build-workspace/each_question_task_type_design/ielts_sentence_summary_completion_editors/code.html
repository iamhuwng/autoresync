The History of the Bicycle

The invention of the bicycle was not a single, isolated event, but rather a gradual evolution over the 19th century. The earliest iteration, known as the 'running machine' or Draisine, was invented by Karl Drais in 1817. This early two-wheeler lacked pedals entirely; riders propelled themselves forward by pushing their feet directly against the ground.

It wasn't until the 1860s that the concept of pedals was introduced by French inventors Pierre Michaux and Pierre Lallement. Their design, often referred to as the velocipede or 'boneshaker', attached pedals directly to the front wheel. This direct-drive system meant that the speed was limited by the size of the wheel, leading to the development of the perilous Penny Farthing, characterized by its massive front wheel and tiny rear wheel.

The introduction of the 'safety bicycle' in the late 1880s by John Kemp Starley was a major turning point. Unlike previous models, it featured two wheels of equal size and utilized a chain to drive the rear wheel. This design vastly improved stability and ease of use, leading to the global bicycle craze of the 1890s. Furthermore, John Boyd Dunlop's invention of the pneumatic tyre in 1888 replaced solid rubber, finally providing a comfortable ride over cobbled streets.
