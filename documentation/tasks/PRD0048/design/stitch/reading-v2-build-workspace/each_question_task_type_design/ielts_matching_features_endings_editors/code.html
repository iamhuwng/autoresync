Urban architecture has undergone a radical transformation over the past century, moving from purely functional structures to intricate designs that attempt to balance high-density living with environmental sustainability. In the early 20th century, the advent of mass-produced steel and elevators led to the proliferation of skyscrapers, fundamentally altering the skyline of major cities.

However, modern architectural theories increasingly emphasize the psychological impact of spaces on their inhabitants. Biophilic design, which incorporates natural lighting, ventilation, and vegetation into the built environment, has seen a resurgence. This approach argues that human beings have an innate connection to nature, and that integrating organic elements into urban landscapes can significantly reduce stress and improve cognitive function.

Critics of contemporary mega-structures argue that they often lack the human scale necessary to foster community interaction. While towering glass edifices symbolize economic prowess, they can also create alienating environments at street level, leading to phenomena such as urban heat islands and wind tunnels.
