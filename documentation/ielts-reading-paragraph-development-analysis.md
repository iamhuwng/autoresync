# IELTS Reading Paragraph Idea-Development Analysis

## Scope and method

This report analyzes the full passage text in 49 numbered files from `Desktop/Clippings/IELTS Reading`: tests 001–019 and 038–067, three passages per test (147 passages). The two non-test Markdown files in that folder are excluded. The separate `Practice Cam 10-20 Passages` folder is a passage index with metadata, not full passage text, so it was used only as a cross-check.

Six parallel reviews were used: five reviews mapped non-overlapping file ranges, and one independent audit stress-tested the coding scheme across a stratified sample. The maps below are compact discourse maps, not content summaries. Letters are source labels; numbers are natural paragraph numbers where labels are absent. A semicolon marks a meaningful within-paragraph turn.

The central finding is that paragraph roles are best taught as **moves in an unfolding argument**, not as fixed types. A paragraph can introduce a concept, give evidence, and qualify that evidence in the same block.

## The two-axis model

### 1. What the paragraph contributes

Use one dominant code and, when needed, one secondary code.

| Code | Function | Student question |
|---|---|---|
| **F — Frame** | Hook, setting, definition, scope, actors, or stakes | What must I know first? |
| **G — Gap / tension** | Puzzle, conflict, unanswered question, unmet need | What now needs explaining or solving? |
| **D — Develop** | Next historical stage, facet, category, or parallel case | Is this another item in a series? |
| **X — Explain** | Cause, mechanism, model, interpretation | Why or how does it happen? |
| **M — Method** | How investigators, designers, or practitioners proceeded | How did they find out or intervene? |
| **E — Evidence / case** | Observation, result, number, named example, or concrete case | What was found or shown? |
| **Q — Qualify / challenge** | Objection, counterclaim, uncertainty, exception, cost, or boundary | What weakens or narrows the claim? |
| **R — Response / evaluation** | Intervention, application, solution, outcome, or assessment | What follows, and does it work? |
| **J — Judgment / outlook** | Synthesis, recommendation, forecast, or deliberate open ending | What conclusion or next question remains? |
| **H — Hinge** | A scale, time, place, or question shift that carries the argument forward | What changed while the topic stayed connected? |

**D** describes the paragraph’s contribution; **time**, **parallel**, **cause**, **support**, **contrast**, **zoom**, and **return** describe its connection to the previous paragraph. Chronology and catalogue are therefore organizing links, not automatically the paragraph’s main function.

### 2. How it connects

For each paragraph, ask whether it:

- continues through **time**;
- adds a **parallel** case or category;
- gives a **cause** or mechanism;
- supplies **support/evidence**;
- introduces **contrast or rebuttal**;
- **zooms** from local to general or general to local;
- **returns** to the opening question after a detour; or
- moves from **problem → response → outcome → limit**.

This prevents two common mistakes: treating every historical paragraph as mere chronology, and treating every named example as the passage’s main idea.

## Sequence templates found repeatedly

1. **Puzzle → inquiry → finding → complication → next question.** The author opens with an anomaly, describes a test, reports a result, then shows why the result is incomplete.
2. **Problem → response → outcome → limitation → revised response.** Common in environmental, health, and policy passages.
3. **Chronology → turning point → consequence → present significance.** Historical accounts often change into a product guide, social explanation, or policy argument near the end.
4. **Claim → exposition → objections → weighed judgment.** The passage alternates support and caveat instead of building a one-sided proof.
5. **Profile / case → evidence → synthesis.** A person, place, or product supplies the credibility or concrete evidence for a wider claim.
6. **Grouped survey.** Frame a domain, move through several sectors or examples, then add a comparison, implication, or recommendation.
7. **Technical design cycle.** Need → mechanism → prototype → test → refinement → adoption or regulation.
8. **Classification tree.** Define the family or term, establish shared traits, then branch into categories and examples.
9. **Detour-and-return.** A long biography, historical episode, or case study temporarily leaves the opening topic and later returns with a stronger interpretation.
10. **Open-ended inquiry.** The passage closes with a live uncertainty, a proposal for further research, or a forecast rather than a neat conclusion.

## A classroom coding routine

1. Give each paragraph a five-word gist before naming its role.
2. Ask “What question does this paragraph answer?” and “What changed from the previous one?”
3. Circle pivot markers such as **but, however, yet, despite, although, on the other hand**; mark **for example, according to, using, to test** as evidence/method signals; mark **therefore, so, this suggests, as a result** as interpretation or implication signals.
4. Separate **finding**, **interpretation**, and **proposal**. A study result is not automatically the author’s conclusion.
5. Read the last sentence again. In this corpus it often contains the qualification, the wider implication, or the next problem.
6. Write the passage route with verbs: “defines → tests → qualifies → applies,” rather than with topic nouns.
7. Only after mapping the text should students use the question types; question order is not the author’s idea-development order.

## Cross-corpus findings

- The dominant structure is **recursive** rather than linear: authors revisit a claim after adding evidence, a counterexample, or a change of scale.
- The strongest paragraph boundaries are often marked by a **change in question**, not a new topic. A new person or site may still serve the same claim.
- Paragraphs frequently combine **evidence + interpretation**, or **response + limitation**. “One paragraph, one function” is a useful starting discipline, not a law.
- Technical and scientific passages commonly separate observation from explanation, then reopen the question when the evidence is ambiguous.
- Historical passages commonly shift organizing principle midway: chronology may become adoption history, engineering analysis, demographic consequence, or present policy.
- Grouped surveys often use additive examples rather than a single proof. Their final paragraph may synthesize, qualify, or simply leave an open future.
- A convincing teaching label should describe the paragraph’s **payoff**, often near the end, not just its opening sentence.
- The clipping corpus contains ads, video-player remnants, typos, duplicated telegraph narratives, unlabelled paragraphs, and incomplete-looking endings. These are source artifacts, not reliable models of authorial intention.

## Teaching cues and cautions

- **Ask what changed between paragraphs.** Dates often mark stages, but the useful relation is *obstacle → solution → new obstacle* (telegraph cables, tea taxation), or *claim → test → qualification* (yawning, climate and wealth).
- **Watch turn words and scope words.** “However,” “yet,” “but,” “despite,” “although,” and “not everyone” commonly signal a limitation or rival account. “For example,” “in this case,” and named studies usually supply evidence; they are rarely the paragraph’s whole purpose. “Therefore” and “this suggests” mark the author’s inference, which may be weaker than the finding.
- **Read paragraph endings.** Many begin with a topic and end by changing its force: 049 P2 D moves from database scale to a contested plateau; 052 P2 B separates emotional vividness from memory accuracy; 057 P3 G turns progress toward eradication into a political obstacle.
- **Use structure, then check it.** Lettered headings and natural paragraph breaks help map the route. One paragraph can contain setup, evidence and a caveat; a section heading can cover several different moves.
- **Treat the files as noisy teaching copies.** They contain ads, glossaries, a table, typos and awkward transcription. Tests 049 P3 and 051 P3 substantially repeat the same telegraph narrative. Teach the rhetorical pattern from the passage text, while treating questionable factual claims and garbled wording as source issues rather than models of good prose.

## Independent audit and coding validation

## Coding taxonomy

Code **what each paragraph contributes**, then code **how it connects to the previous paragraph**. This keeps a chronology or a list of examples from being mistaken for an argument.

| Code | Paragraph function | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **F Frame** | Introduces the topic, setting, actors, definition, or stakes | What must the reader know first? |
| **G Gap** | Poses an unanswered question, conflict, or unmet need | What now needs explaining or solving? |
| **D Develop** | Adds the next historical stage, facet, or parallel case | Is this another item in a series? |
| **X Explain** | Gives a cause, mechanism, theory, or interpretation | Why or how does it happen? |
| **M Method** | Describes how investigators or practitioners proceeded | What did they do to find out or intervene? |
| **E Evidence** | Reports observations, findings, numbers, or a supporting case | What was found or shown? |
| **Q Qualify** | Challenges, limits, complicates, or offers a rival view | What weakens or narrows the preceding claim? |
| **R Response** | Describes an intervention, application, consequence, or recommendation | What follows, or what can be done? |
| **J Judgment** | Weighs the account, synthesizes it, evaluates it, or leaves a forecast | What conclusion or uncertainty remains? |

Useful **connection labels** are *time*, *parallel*, *zoom in/out*, *cause*, *support*, *contrast*, and *return*. Code these separately. For example, 057 P1’s first four paragraphs move **through time**; 062 P3 moves between **parallel institutions**; 038 P2 E→F is **objection→concession/rebuttal**.

## Limit of inference

The passage maps are a teaching corpus analysis of these saved clippings, not a statistical claim about every IELTS text. The coding scheme is designed for transfer: students should test it against the full passage and allow mixed or open-ended paragraphs where the text requires it.
